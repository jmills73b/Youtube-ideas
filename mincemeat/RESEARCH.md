# Operation Mincemeat, 1943: research notes

These notes sit behind the narration (`script.py`) and the on-screen text. Each claim was checked against published sources: histories, museum sites and archive transcriptions (see the bibliography). Where accounts differ, the notes say so and explain the choice made.

Documents shown on screen are **reconstructions**. Quoted words are taken from published transcriptions; letterheads, layout and handwriting are illustrative. Where the film summarises rather than quotes, the text is in [square brackets].

## 1. Strategic setting

| Claim | Detail / source |
|---|---|
| North Africa almost won, early 1943 | Axis forces in Tunisia surrendered on 13 May 1943. The Casablanca Conference (January 1943) chose Sicily as the next target (Operation Husky). |
| "Everybody but a bloody fool would know that it's Sicily" | Widely **attributed** to Churchill; the wording varies ("anybody but a damned fool…"). The film says "as Churchill is said to have put it" and labels the quote as attributed. |
| Deception needed | Mincemeat was one strand of **Operation Barclay**, the wider deception plan to make the Germans expect landings in Greece and the Balkans, and in Sardinia and Corsica. |

## 2. The idea

- **The Trout Memo** (29 September 1939) was issued under Rear-Admiral John Godfrey, Director of Naval Intelligence. It is generally credited to his personal assistant, Ian Fleming. It compares deception to trout fishing and lists about 50 schemes.
- Item 28 is headed "a suggestion (not a very nice one)". It proposes that "a corpse dressed as an airman, with despatches in his pockets, could be dropped on the coast". The idea came from Basil Thomson's 1937 novel *The Milliner's Hat Mystery*.
- The on-screen memo quotes only these widely reproduced phrases; the rest is summarised in brackets.
- **The planners.** Flight Lieutenant Charles Cholmondeley (MI5, B1a) and Lieutenant Commander Ewen Montagu (Naval Intelligence, NID 17M) developed the plan in early 1943 under the Twenty (XX) Committee. Much of the work was done in Room 13 of the Admiralty.

## 3. Glyndwr Michael

| Claim | Detail / source |
|---|---|
| Identity | Glyndwr Michael, born 4 January 1909 in Aberbargoed, Monmouthshire (now Caerphilly county borough), Wales. |
| Death | Found in a disused warehouse near King's Cross on 26 January 1943, having swallowed rat poison containing phosphorus. He died at St Pancras Hospital on 28 January 1943. (Some sources give 24 January; the film says only "January 1943".) Whether it was suicide or accident is unknown. |
| The coroner | Sir Bentley Purchase, St Pancras coroner, provided the body without the usual formalities. Phosphorus poisoning was hard to tell from drowning at a casual post-mortem. |
| No family looking for him | His parents were dead. The identity was kept secret until research published in 1996 (Roger Morgan). The CWGC recognised him in 1998. |

Pronunciation: *Glyndŵr* is said roughly "glin-DOOR".

## 4. Major William Martin

- **Identity.** Captain (Acting Major) William Martin, Royal Marines, on the staff of Combined Operations HQ. A naval identity card was made up with a photograph of MI5 officer Ronnie Reed (not shown in the film).
- **"Wallet litter"**
  - Two love letters and a photograph from his fiancée "Pam". The photograph was of MI5 clerk **Jean Leslie**; the letters are credited to Hester Leggett, head of the secretarial section.
  - A bill from S.J. Phillips of Bond Street for a diamond engagement ring.
  - A letter from Lloyds Bank about an overdraft of £79 19s 2d.
  - Two ticket stubs for the Prince of Wales Theatre dated 22 April 1943. These proved he had left London after that date.
  - Also, among others: a letter from his "father", and a bill from the Naval and Military Club.
- **On screen.** The letters, bills and tickets are reconstructions; wording beyond the facts above is omitted or illustrative. The "Pam" photograph is a stylised drawing, not a copy of the real portrait.

## 5. The letters

| Letter | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Nye to Alexander** | Lieutenant-General Sir Archibald Nye (Vice-Chief of the Imperial General Staff) wrote a personal letter to General Sir Harold Alexander, dated **23 April 1943** from the War Office and signed "Archie Nye". It opens: "My dear Alex, I am taking advantage of sending you a personal letter by hand of one of Mountbatten's officers, to give you the inside history of our recent exchange of cables about Mediterranean operations and their attendant cover plans." These words are shown verbatim. The letter implied landings in **Greece** (it mentions Cape Araxos and Kalamata), with **Sicily** as the cover target for another operation. This part is summarised in brackets on screen. |
| **Mountbatten to Cunningham** | Vice-Admiral Lord Louis Mountbatten (Chief of Combined Operations) wrote to Admiral of the Fleet Sir Andrew Cunningham, dated **21 April 1943**, introducing Martin. It ends with the joke "He might bring some sardines with him — they are 'on points' here!", which pointed to Sardinia. That line is shown verbatim. |

## 6. HMS Seraph and the release

- The body was dressed on 17 April 1943 and packed in dry ice in a steel canister. That night it was driven from London to Greenock by MI5 driver and racing driver **St John "Jock" Horsfall**, with Montagu and Cholmondeley, arriving on 18 April.
- **HMS *Seraph***, an S-class submarine under Lieutenant **Norman "Bill" Jewell**, sailed from Holy Loch on the Clyde on **19 April 1943**. The track on the map is schematic.
- **At about 04:30 on 30 April 1943**, off Huelva, the canister was opened on deck. Jewell read **Psalm 39**, and the body was put into the sea with an inflated life jacket. An upturned dinghy was released separately. The crew had been told the canister held meteorological equipment; only the officers knew the truth.

## 7. Huelva

- The body was found on 30 April 1943 by the sardine fisherman José Antonio Rey María, off Punta Umbría near Huelva.
- The Abwehr's man in Huelva was **Adolf Clauss**. The British vice-consul was Francis Haselden.
- **Burial.** Major Martin was buried on **2 May 1943** in the cemetery of Nuestra Señora de la Soledad, Huelva, with Spanish military honours.
- **The papers.** Spanish officials passed the documents to the Germans, who photographed them. The envelopes were resealed and returned to the British on 13 May. Examination in London showed they had been opened.

## 8. "Swallowed"

- Ultra decrypts (Bletchley Park) showed that the German high command accepted the papers. A German intelligence appreciation judged them genuine.
- **The telegram.** A message to Churchill, who was then in Washington for the Trident conference, read "MINCEMEAT SWALLOWED ROD, LINE AND SINKER". Published accounts vary slightly in wording and punctuation.
- **German moves.** On 14 May 1943 Hitler told Dönitz that the landings would come in Sardinia and the Peloponnese. The **1st Panzer Division** was ordered from France to Greece, and defences in Greece, Sardinia and Corsica were strengthened. Later that summer Rommel was sent to Greece.
- Some popular accounts give much larger troop movements attributed to Mincemeat, such as "ten divisions to the Balkans". The film does not use these figures, because they mix Mincemeat with the wider Barclay deception and with moves made for other reasons.

## 9. Sicily and the assessment

- **The landings.** Operation Husky landed on 10 July 1943, with airborne landings on the night of 9/10 July. Sicily was taken by **17 August 1943**, when the Allies entered Messina: a campaign of 38 days.
- **Surprise.** The Allies achieved substantial surprise; casualties at the landings were lower than feared.
- **Effectiveness.** Sir Michael Howard, in *Strategic Deception in the Second World War* (volume 5 of the official history of British intelligence), called Mincemeat "the best known, and perhaps the most successful single deception operation of the entire war". The film quotes the second part.
- **Historians' caution.** Some historians warn that Mincemeat's exact contribution is impossible to measure. The Germans also reinforced Sicily itself, and other factors, including the wider Barclay plan, mattered. The narration therefore says "not the only reason".

## 10. The grave

- Grave 1886, San Marco section, Nuestra Señora de la Soledad cemetery, Huelva. It is maintained by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission.
- **Original inscription:** "WILLIAM MARTIN / BORN 29TH MARCH 1907 / DIED 24TH APRIL 1943 / BELOVED SON OF JOHN GLYNDWYR MARTIN AND THE LATE ANTONIA MARTIN OF CARDIFF, WALES / DULCE ET DECORUM EST PRO PATRIA MORI / R.I.P."
- **Added in 1998:** "GLYNDWR MICHAEL; SERVED AS MAJOR WILLIAM MARTIN, RM".
- The drawing of the headstone is simplified; the wording follows the published inscription.

## 11. Visual research notes

- **Picture looks.** Newsreel shots follow the look of 35 mm black-and-white British cinema newsreels of 1943: soft grain, gentle flicker and vignette. The plot scenes use a harder, "noir" black and white. The final shot of the grave is in colour, as the present day.
- **Maps** imitate 1940s War Office and newspaper war maps. They use real coastlines (Natural Earth 10 m). Solid arrows show real moves; dashed arrows show the threats the Germans were meant to believe in.
- **Wartime details**
  - The van shows the blackout-masked lamps of wartime roads.
  - HMS *Seraph* is drawn as an S-class submarine: bridge fin, periscope standards, and the 3-inch gun forward of the tower.
- **Restraint.** The body itself is never depicted, out of respect for Glyndwr Michael. It is represented by the canister, the briefcase and the life jacket at a distance.
