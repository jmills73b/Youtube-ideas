# The Battle of the Somme, 1916: research notes

These notes sit behind the narration (`script.py`) and the on-screen text. Every
figure used in the film is listed here with its source. Where historians
disagree, the notes give the range and say why the film uses the figure it does.

Method: each claim was checked against at least one institutional or specialist source. These include the Imperial War Museums, the National Army Museum, the Commonwealth War Graves Commission (CWGC), Veterans Affairs Canada, the Australian War Memorial, the Tank Museum and the Long, Long Trail. Where these conflicted, standard published histories were used as a cross-check (see the bibliography). Casualty figures are always given as the film's rounded or "about" figure, with the more precise number noted here.

## 1. Strategic background

| Claim in film | Detail and source |
|---|---|
| Western Front ran from the Channel coast to Switzerland | Continuous trench line from the Belgian coast near Nieuport to the Swiss frontier near Pfetterhouse from late 1914. |
| Chantilly, December 1915 | Second Inter-Allied Conference at Chantilly, 6–8 Dec 1915 (France, Britain, Russia, Italy, Serbia, Belgium). Agreed simultaneous offensives for 1916. The Anglo-French offensive on the Somme was fixed in talks between Joffre and Haig early in 1916. Sources: The Long, Long Trail ("political and strategic background"); FirstWorldWar.com (Chantilly memorandum). |
| Germany attacked Verdun in February | 21 February 1916. |
| Somme became mainly British, with the aim of relieving Verdun | The French contribution was cut from roughly 40 divisions planned to about 13 on 1 July. Relief of Verdun became a stated aim. Sources: The Long, Long Trail; Philpott, *Bloody Victory*. |

## 2. Kitchener's Army and the Pals

- Lord Kitchener, Secretary of State for War, called for volunteers from August 1914; about 2.5 million men volunteered before conscription (January 1916).
- "Pals" battalions were raised locally from men of the same town, workplace or club. On 1 July many of these New Army battalions fought their first major action.
- The recruiting poster in the film is a composite in the style of the period. Its wording ("Your King & Country Need You", "A Call to Arms", "God Save the King") follows actual 1914 posters. It is **not** a reproduction of any single poster.

## 3. The bombardment

| Claim | Figure used | Source / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Start | 24 June 1916 | Long, Long Trail; History Hit. |
| Shells | "more than one and a half million" | Usually given as c. 1.5–1.7 million, fired 24 June – 1 July. IWM gives "over 1.5 million". |
| Duration | seven days (on screen) | The assault was planned for 29 June and postponed 48 hours for bad weather, so the bombardment ran to 1 July. |
| Shrapnel | "most were shrapnel" | Around two-thirds were shrapnel, which was poor at cutting thick wire and useless against deep dugouts (IWM; NAM). |
| Duds | "as many as a third failed to explode" | Estimates run to about 30% (IWM: "as many as 30 per cent"). |
| Wire and dugouts | wire largely uncut; Germans sheltered in deep dugouts | IWM; NAM. The film's cross-section is labelled *schematic*. |

## 6. The cost

| Claim | Figure used | Notes and range |
|---|---|---|
| Length of battle | 141 days | 1 July – 18 November 1916. |
| Furthest Allied advance | "no more than seven miles" | Commonly given as about 6–7 miles (about 10–12 km) at the deepest point. |
| British Empire casualties | "some 420,000" | Official History figure, c. 419,654; the round figure of 420,000 is standard (IWM, NAM). |
| French casualties | "about 200,000" | c. 202,567 (French sources); commonly 195,000–204,000. |
| German casualties | "at least 450,000" | **Contested.** Modern estimates run from about 430,000 to 600,000, and several historians accept 465,000–500,000. The British official historian Edmonds claimed 680,000, a figure since discredited. "At least 450,000" is the conservative wording used by the NAM and similar summaries. |

"Casualties" throughout means killed, wounded, missing and captured, as the on-screen card states.

## 7. Legacy and historiography

- **The debate.** The interpretation that it was a "futile slaughter" (the "lions led by donkeys" school; e.g. Alan Clark, *The Donkeys*, 1961; A.J.P. Taylor) contrasts with a "learning curve" and attrition view, in which the battle wore down the German army and taught the British Army lessons applied in 1917–18 (e.g. Gary Sheffield, *The Somme*; William Philpott, *Bloody Victory*). The film states the question and does not take sides.
- **The quotation** "The Somme was the muddy grave of the German field army" is from Captain Hans von Hentig of the General Staff of the Guards Reserve Division. It appears in his *Psychologische Strategie des großen Krieges* (1927) and is often quoted in English (e.g. by Sheffield). The full sentence continues: "…and of the faith in the infallibility of the German leadership, dug by British industry and its shells." Its precise wording in translation varies.
- **German withdrawal.** Falkenhayn was replaced as Chief of the General Staff by Hindenburg (with Ludendorff) on 29 August 1916. Operation Alberich, the withdrawal to the Hindenburg Line (Siegfriedstellung), ran from 9 February to 20 March 1917 and shortened the German front by about 40 km. The film's map traces the Hindenburg Line schematically between Arras and Soissons.
- **Thiepval Memorial.** Designed by Sir Edwin Lutyens, built 1928–32 and unveiled 1 August 1932. It bears the names of more than 72,000 men of the United Kingdom and South African forces who died on the Somme before 20 March 1918 and have no known grave (CWGC gives 72,000+; counts of 72,195–72,337 appear as panels are amended). The drawing in the film simplifies the stepped, interlocking arches and shows the Anglo-French cemetery in front.
- **Poppies.** The red field poppy (*Papaver rhoeas*) grew on the disturbed soil of the Western Front and became the symbol of remembrance after John McCrae's poem "In Flanders Fields" (1915).

## 8. Visual research notes

- **The 1916 look.** Shots are graded like surviving prints of the official film *The Battle of the Somme* (Malins and McDowell, released 21 August 1916 and seen by some 20 million people in its first six weeks). The grade is monochrome with a sepia tint, a blue tint for night (as in period tinted prints), heavy flicker, gate weave, dust, hairs and scratches. Motion steps at about 16 frames a second, like a hand-cranked camera. Intertitles use IM Fell, a revival of 17th-century English type similar to the serif lettering on 1916 title cards.
- **Maps** imitate British trench maps of 1916: squared grid, lettered squares, and German trenches printed in red. The river Somme, place names and village positions come from real data: Natural Earth rivers, and French commune boundaries (IGN, via france-geojson), used as commune centroids. The Ancre is traced down its valley by hand. **Front lines are schematic.** They follow the documented village-by-village progress for 1 July, 14 July, late September and 18 November; they are not surveyed trench lines.
- **Equipment**
  - British figures wear the Brodie Mk I steel helmet; German figures wear the M1916 Stahlhelm. It was introduced at Verdun early in 1916 and issued to more units during the Somme battle, so in July many Germans still wore the Pickelhaube or a field cap; the helmet is used here as a recognisable symbol. Volunteers in 1914–15 scenes wear service dress caps.
  - The tank is a Mark I Male: rhomboid hull, all-round tracks, 6-pounder guns in side sponsons, and the twin-wheel steering tail fitted in 1916. It carries the hand-painted camouflage used at Flers.
  - The guns shown are the 18-pounder field gun and heavy howitzers.
- **Mines** are shown as eyewitnesses described them: the ground heaves, then a tall column of earth and white chalk rises.
- **Dugouts** were cut into the Somme chalk, often 20–40 ft deep; the drawing is labelled schematic.
